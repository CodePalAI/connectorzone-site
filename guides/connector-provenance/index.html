Reading provenance in the MCP registry

What a namespace proves, how to spot bulk publishing, and why the catalog caps how many entries one publisher can contribute.

6 minute read. Updated 2026-07-30.

The registry contains more than nineteen thousand records. A meaningful share of them come from a small number of publishers who submit in bulk. Knowing how to tell the difference between a maintained project and an automatically generated entry is most of what provenance review is.

What the namespace proves

Every record sits under a reverse domain namespace that was verified before publication.

io.github.someone/thing means someone proved control of the someone GitHub account at publish time.

com.example/thing means someone proved control of the example.com domain, usually through DNS or an HTTP challenge.

The domain form is stronger, because registering a domain and proving control of it is a higher bar than creating an account. It is stronger again when the domain matches the website on the same record, which is the pairing we label as vendor published across the catalog.

What neither form proves is that the software is good, maintained or safe. The registry moderation policy is intentionally light touch and says so.

Spotting bulk submissions

Bulk publishing is legal, common and easy to see once you know the pattern.

One namespace, hundreds of records. In the current snapshot the largest single publisher accounts for over a thousand records. That is not inherently wrong, and it does mean those records were almost certainly generated rather than written.

Descriptions that repeat. Generated records tend to share phrasing across a publisher catalog. When the same sentence appears on twenty records with a different noun swapped in, you are reading a template.

No repository, no website. A record with neither is a record nobody expects you to read. We refuse to build a profile page for those at all.

Everything published on the same day. The registry records a publish timestamp. A thousand records with the same timestamp is a script.

What this catalog does about it

Three rules, applied to every record, documented on the methodology page.

A per publisher ceiling. No publisher contributes more than twenty four profile pages, ranked by completeness. The intent is not to punish prolific authors, it is to stop one generated catalog from becoming most of the directory.

Duplicate detection. Records whose description matches another record word for word are kept out of search indexing. They stay browsable, because they might still be the connector you want, and they do not deserve a search result of their own.

A reference requirement. No repository and no website means no profile page. If nothing on a page could be verified by a reader, the page should not exist.

Reading a record yourself

The registry schema is public, and every field on our profile pages maps to something in it. When you want to check what we say, the schema tells you what each field means and our raw JSON link gives you the record we normalised.

Four fields carry most of the signal:

  • repository tells you whether there is anything to read.
  • websiteUrl tells you whether a product exists behind the code.
  • packages[].version tells you whether the publisher pins releases.
  • _meta publish and update timestamps tell you whether anyone still touches this.

Aggregators and copies

The registry publishes guidance for aggregators, which is the category this site falls into. The relevant part for a reader is that any catalog built on registry data is a copy taken at a moment in time.

Ours states its moment openly. The data status page shows when the snapshot was taken, how many records it holds and the digest that identifies it. If a connector was published after that timestamp it will not be here yet, and that is a data freshness limit rather than an editorial judgement.

The limits of every registry signal

Provenance answers one question well: who published this. It does not answer whether the code does what it says, whether the publisher will still be maintaining it next year, or whether the tools it exposes are safe to hand to an autonomous agent.

For those, read the repository, read the security guide, and scope the credential to the smallest thing that works.
